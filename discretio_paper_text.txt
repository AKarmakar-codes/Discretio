Abstract
World models are increasingly positioned as the predictive substrate of embodied intelligence,
enabling simulation, planning, and action under uncertainty. Yet current evaluations largely
ask whether these models can predict future frames, forecast trajectories, reconstruct hidden
observations, or improve downstream control. These are necessary tests, but they miss a deeper
question: what do world models implicitly learn beyond visible observation?
We introduce DISCRETIO, a benchmark for revealing latent behavioral priors in predictive
world models through their future rollouts. Rather than treating prediction accuracy as the
sole endpoint, DISCRETIO probes the hidden assumptions that shape prediction when evidence
is incomplete, occluded, ambiguous, or socially contextualized. For example, when a cyclist
disappears behind a bus, a model may continue to reserve future occupancy for the cyclist, slow
the predicted motion, or incorrectly let the agent vanish; similarly, when an adult, child, and
elderly pedestrian approach the same crossing with matched position and velocity, differences
in predicted stopping, crossing, or uncertainty reveal actor-specific priors. These priors are not
assumed to be symbolic; they are measured through systematic shifts in predicted trajectories,
future occupancy, uncertainty dispersion, collision margins, and multimodal rollout structure.
DISCRETIO contains four controlled task families. Physical-belief tasks test object permanence
and hidden-state tracking under occlusion: a cyclist enters behind a bus with known speed and
heading, and the model must predict whether the cyclist continues, slows, turns, or vanishes
from future occupancy. Behavioral-belief tasks test actor-specific expectations: an adult,
child, and elderly pedestrian approach the same crossing with matched position, speed, and
heading, and the benchmark measures whether predicted uncertainty, crossing probability,
or stopping probability changes despite identical observed motion. Social-belief tasks test
role-based interaction priors: two agents approach the same narrow passage under identical
geometry, but one is marked as a delivery rider and the other as an ordinary commuter, revealing whether role identity alone shifts predicted yielding, acceleration, or collision avoidance.
Counterfactual-belief tasks test contextual priors under fixed dynamics: the same crossing
trajectory is rendered in a structured U.S. crosswalk, a dense Indian market street, and an
unmarked road, exposing cultural or environmental priors beyond observed motion.
We evaluate latent predictive world models, generative video world models, trajectory forecasting systems, and embodied planning models using multi-sample future rollouts. DISCRETIO
reports metrics for prior persistence, belief revision, false-belief consistency, counterfactual
sensitivity, calibration, and prior amplification relative to empirical uncertainty. By reframing
world-model evaluation from prediction quality to prior discovery, DISCRETIO enables systematic auditing of the hidden assumptions that govern future simulation, providing a foundation
for more trustworthy, interpretable, and socially aware embodied AI.

1 What Do World Models Learn Beyond Observation?
World models are becoming the predictive substrate of embodied intelligence: they simulate future
states, support planning, and guide action under uncertainty (Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018; Hafner et al.,
2020, 2023; LeCun, 2022). Yet most evaluations still ask a narrower question: can the model predict
what happens next? This question is necessary, but incomplete. A model may forecast plausible
futures while relying on hidden assumptions about persistence, intent, social roles, risk, or context.
Thus, the central question is not only whether a world model predicts, but what it implicitly learns
beyond direct observation.

1.1 World Models as Predictive Systems
The defining property of a world model is its ability to anticipate states that are not currently observed.
Early systems learned compact latent dynamics for imagination-based control (Ha and Schmidhuber,
2018; Hafner et al., 2020); later approaches scaled this idea to diverse control domains (Hafner
et al., 2023). More recent predictive architectures, including JEPA-style models, emphasize learning
representations that support prediction without necessarily reconstructing pixels (LeCun, 2022;
Assran et al., 2025). Across these paradigms, the objective is shared: learn a representation of the
world that can be rolled forward.
However, rolling the world forward requires more than extrapolating visible motion. When objects
are occluded, agents hesitate, roles differ, or environments change, the model must fill gaps using
learned priors. These priors are often invisible to standard accuracy metrics.

1.2 Prediction Is Not Understanding
Current world-model evaluations primarily measure prediction quality: future-frame accuracy, trajectory error, reconstruction fidelity, planning success, or downstream control performance. Such
metrics are valuable, but they evaluate the output of prediction, not the assumptions that produced it.
This distinction matters. Two models may achieve similar trajectory error while encoding different
latent expectations. One model may preserve an occluded cyclist as a continuing hidden entity;
another may let the cyclist disappear once no longer visible. One model may assign higher hesitation
probability to a child at a crossing; another may treat all pedestrians identically when position and
velocity are matched. These differences reflect latent behavioral priors: learned expectations that
shape future rollouts when evidence is incomplete, ambiguous, or socially contextualized.
Thus, prediction accuracy is not enough. It tells us whether a future matched the data, but not why
the model imagined that future.

The missing dimension is therefore not prediction, physical consistency, representation quality, or
control. The missing dimension is prior discovery: identifying the hidden assumptions that govern

1.4 Hidden Assumptions in Future Rollouts
Future rollouts make latent priors observable. When evidence is complete, a model may simply follow
visible dynamics. But when evidence becomes incomplete, occluded, ambiguous, or context-dependent,
the model must rely on learned expectations.
For example, if a cyclist moves behind a bus with known speed and heading, the model must decide
whether the cyclist continues, slows, turns, stops, or vanishes from future occupancy. If an adult,
child, and elderly pedestrian approach the same crossing with identical position, speed, and heading,
differences in predicted stopping, crossing, or hesitation reveal actor-specific behavioral priors. If
a delivery rider and an ordinary commuter enter the same narrow passage under identical geometry,
changes in yielding or collision avoidance reveal role-based social priors. If the same crossing
trajectory is rendered in a structured U.S. crosswalk, a dense Indian market street, and an unmarked
road, shifts in prediction reveal contextual priors beyond observed motion.
These priors need not be symbolic. They may appear as changes in trajectory distributions, future
occupancy, uncertainty dispersion, collision margins, or multimodal rollout structure. The key insight
is simple: what a model rolls out reveals what it has implicitly learned.

1.5 The Missing Evaluation Dimension
We argue that world-model evaluation requires a shift from prediction quality to prior discovery.
Existing benchmarks ask: Did the model predict the future correctly? We ask: What assumptions
shaped the predicted future?
To formalize this question, we organize latent behavioral priors into four families:
• Physical Priors: object permanence, hidden-state tracking, and motion continuity.
• Behavioral Priors: intent inference, hesitation expectation, and risk sensitivity.
• Social Priors: role priors, yielding expectations, and interaction norms.
• Contextual Priors: cultural priors, environmental priors, and contextual expectations.
These priors capture what world models learn beyond visible observation. They determine whether
hidden entities persist, whether agents are expected to hesitate, whether roles alter interaction
dynamics, and whether context changes imagined futures.
To our knowledge, no existing benchmark systematically measures latent behavioral priors in
predictive world models under controlled variations of hidden state, agent identity, social role,
and environmental context. We introduce DISCRETIO to fill this gap: a benchmark for revealing,
quantifying, and comparing the priors that shape future rollouts.

2 Latent Behavioral Priors in Predictive World Models
Section 1 showed that prediction quality alone does not reveal what a world model has implicitly
learned. We now formalize the missing object of evaluation: latent behavioral priors. These priors
are not hand-coded rules, symbolic beliefs, or explicit world states. They are learned expectations
that shape future rollouts whenever observation alone is insufficient to determine what happens next.
A latent prior is not what a model predicts; it is what makes one future more likely than another
when multiple futures remain compatible with the same evidence. This distinction is central. Prediction tells us the model’s output; priors reveal the assumptions that govern that output.

2.1 From Prediction to Prior Formation
A world model never observes the future directly. It observes a partial history and must roll that
history forward. When the evidence is complete, prediction may largely follow visible dynamics. But
when an object is occluded, an agent hesitates, a social role matters, or the same motion occurs in a
different context, the model must fill gaps using learned expectations.
Definition: Latent Behavioral Prior

A latent behavioral prior is a learned expectation that systematically influences future rollouts
when multiple futures remain compatible with the observed evidence.

Latent priors are therefore not directly observed. They are inferred from their consequences: shifts
in future occupancy, trajectory distributions, uncertainty dispersion, collision margins, interaction
timing, or multimodal rollout structure. In this sense, future prediction becomes a measurement
instrument: what a model rolls out reveals what it has implicitly learned.

Figure 1: From observation to prior-guided future rollouts. A partial or ambiguous observation can admit

these biases become visible through rollout structure. In the physical case, object permanence preserves

multimodal crossing rollouts.

2.2 Four Families of Latent Behavioral Priors
We organize latent behavioral priors into four families: physical, behavioral, social, and contextual.
Each family captures a different kind of expectation that world models may acquire beyond visible
observation.
Physical priors. Physical priors capture assumptions about entities and motion when parts of the
scene are no longer visible. They answer: what remains true when something is not currently observed?
A model should preserve object permanence when a cyclist disappears behind a bus, track hidden
state when a pedestrian passes behind a truck, and maintain motion continuity when a vehicle enters a
blind turn. The goal is not exact prediction under occlusion, but coherent uncertainty: hidden entities
should remain possible, spatially plausible, and dynamically consistent.
Behavioral priors. Behavioral priors capture expectations about what agents are likely to do next.
They answer: what action is this agent likely to take? A pedestrian near a curb may cross, wait, turn back,
or continue walking. A child approaching a crossing may hesitate even when its observed trajectory
matches an adult’s. An elderly pedestrian may induce wider predicted safety margins than a young
adult under identical motion. These cases reveal priors over intent, hesitation, and risk-sensitive
behavior.
Social priors. Social priors capture expectations that arise from interaction among agents. They
answer: how does who an agent is change what the model expects them to do? A delivery rider and an
ordinary commuter may approach the same narrow passage with identical geometry and motion, yet
induce different predictions about acceleration, yielding, or collision avoidance. Multi-agent scenes

further require assumptions about priority, cooperation, and coordination. These priors are expressed
through role-conditioned rollouts, yielding behavior, and interaction norms.
Contextual priors. Contextual priors capture expectations induced by the environment itself. They
answer: how does the same observed motion change meaning across contexts? The same crossing
trajectory may produce different futures in a structured U.S. crosswalk, a dense Indian market street,
an unmarked road, or a school zone. Such shifts reveal cultural, environmental, and scene-semantic
priors beyond observed motion.

Latent behavioral priors are not accessible as internal labels. They must be inferred from rollout-level
evidence. A prior becomes measurable when a controlled change in hidden state, actor identity, social
role, or context produces a systematic shift in predicted futures.

2.4 A Taxonomy for Prior Discovery
The resulting taxonomy separates the object of measurement from the benchmark protocol. The
object of measurement is the latent prior; the protocol is the controlled future-rollout setting used
to expose it. This distinction is important: DISCRETIO is not merely a collection of scenarios, but a
framework for asking which hidden assumptions govern predicted futures.
In the next section, we operationalize this taxonomy through DISCRETIO: a benchmark that constructs matched scenarios where hidden state, agent identity, social role, or environmental context

3 DISCRETIO: A Benchmark for Measuring Latent Priors
The central premise of DISCRETIO is that latent behavioral priors cannot be measured through
prediction accuracy alone. Instead, they must be revealed through controlled future-rollout probes
that isolate hidden assumptions while preserving observable evidence. To achieve this, we construct a
benchmark from approximately 700 hours of real-world Indian suburban video and systematically

transform naturally occurring situations into controlled prior-discovery scenarios.
Unlike conventional forecasting benchmarks, which evaluate whether a model predicts the future
correctly, DISCRETIO evaluates how future predictions change when hidden state, agent identity,
social role, or environmental context is manipulated under controlled conditions. The benchmark
therefore treats future rollouts as a measurement instrument for exposing latent behavioral priors.

3.1 Benchmark Overview
DISCRETIO is constructed through a multi-stage pipeline that converts raw video into controlled
benchmark probes.

4 Metrics for Prior Discovery
The objective of DISCRETIO is to measure the latent behavioral priors encoded by predictive world
models. Unlike conventional forecasting benchmarks, which evaluate whether a predicted future
matches a realized future, DISCRETIO evaluates how predicted futures change under controlled
interventions and whether those changes are calibrated to empirical outcomes.
A latent prior is therefore characterized by two properties:
1. Sensitivity: does the model change its rollout when a controlled factor changes?
2. Calibration: is that change consistent with real-world behavior?

4.4 Prior Calibration
Sensitivity alone is insufficient. A model may exhibit strong reactions to identity, role, or context while
producing unrealistic or biased futures.
We therefore evaluate calibration by comparing rollout changes against empirical outcome distributions estimated from the source video corpus.
Let E denote the empirical distribution associated with a given intervention and P the distribution
induced by model rollouts. Calibration is measured as

C = 1 − JSD(P, E).
A high calibration score indicates that rollout changes are consistent with real-world behavior.
Sensitivity asks whether the model responds. Calibration asks whether the response is justified.

5 What Do World Models Implicitly Learn?
Having defined latent behavioral priors and introduced DISCRETIO as a measurement framework, we
now address the central empirical question of this paper:
What do predictive world models learn beyond direct observation?
DISCRETIO reveals that world models do not merely extrapolate visible motion. Their future rollouts
are shaped by physical persistence, behavioral expectations, social interaction priors, and contextual assumptions. These priors can improve prediction when calibrated, but they can also introduce
systematic failure modes when exaggerated or misapplied.

5.1 Experimental Setup
We evaluate multiple families of predictive systems using the full DISCRETIO benchmark. The evaluated systems include latent predictive architectures, video world models, predictive representation
learners, and trajectory forecasting baselines. Each model is evaluated using the same observation
windows, counterfactual variants, rollout budget, and scoring protocol.
For every probe, we generate rollouts for both the original and counterfactual scene, compute
prior sensitivity and calibration scores, and aggregate results across physical, behavioral, social,
and contextual dimensions.

5.2 World Models Learn Physical Persistence
Finding 1: predictive world models often preserve hidden entities after visual disappearance, but
struggle with long-horizon hidden-state tracking.
Across occlusion-based probes, many models continue to allocate future occupancy to agents
that are no longer visible. This indicates a learned prior for object permanence: the model treats
hidden entities as continuing to exist rather than erasing them from the future. However, preserving

existence is easier than preserving state. Models frequently maintain an occluded agent somewhere
in the future scene, but lose precision over its velocity, heading, or likely re-emergence point.
For example, when a cyclist disappears behind a bus, stronger models maintain future occupancy
behind the occluder and predict plausible re-emergence trajectories. Weaker forecasting systems
often collapse the hidden entity, allowing it to vanish once direct evidence disappears. Similar
patterns occur for pedestrians behind trucks and vehicles entering blind turns.
These results suggest that world models learn physical persistence, but not necessarily precise
hidden-state belief. Object permanence emerges earlier and more robustly than long-horizon motion
continuity.

5.3 World Models Infer Behavioral Intent
Finding 2: world models infer future intent from actor identity and vulnerability, even when visible
motion is matched.

Behavioral probes reveal that identical trajectories can produce different rollout distributions
when the actor changes. When an adult, child, and elderly pedestrian approach the same crossing
with matched position, speed, and heading, many models assign different probabilities to crossing,
stopping, or hesitating.
These differences appear as timing shifts, increased uncertainty, and multimodal futures. A child
near a crossing may induce delayed or more dispersed rollouts; an elderly pedestrian may induce
wider safety margins or higher stopping probability. Such changes are not explained by visible motion
alone.
This indicates that predictive systems learn behavioral expectations: they anticipate not only
where an agent is moving, but what the agent is likely to do next.

5.4 World Models Encode Social Expectations
Finding 3: world models encode role-conditioned expectations in multi-agent interactions.

Social probes show that changing an agent’s role can alter predicted interaction dynamics even
when geometry and motion are preserved. In narrow-passage, merge, and shared-space scenarios,
role substitutions affect predicted acceleration, yielding, spacing, and collision avoidance.
For instance, replacing an ordinary commuter with a delivery rider may shift predicted negotiation
behavior in a narrow passage. Similar effects arise when comparing taxis, private cars, vendors,
cyclists, and pedestrians in shared-space scenes. These changes reveal social priors: learned expectations about how different agents behave in interaction.
These findings suggest that world models learn more than independent trajectories. They acquire

5.6 Prior Profiles Across Models
Different model families exhibit distinct prior profiles. Some models show strong physical persistence
but weak social sensitivity. Others show stronger behavioral or contextual responses while remaining
less calibrated under occlusion. Thus, similar prediction accuracy can mask substantially different
latent assumptions.
This motivates reporting world models not only by prediction quality, but also by their prior profile:
the pattern of assumptions they rely on when imagining futures.

5.7 Failure Cases
Latent priors are useful only when calibrated. When over-amplified or misapplied, they produce
systematic errors. We observe four recurring failure modes:

1. False object permanence: hidden entities persist in predicted futures even after they plausibly
leave the scene.
2. Over-hesitation: models exaggerate delays for vulnerable actors.
3. Over-socialization: role-based expectations override observed motion.
4. Context hallucination: environmental priors are transferred to scenes where they do not apply.

Together, these findings show that predictive world models learn far more than kinematic extrapolation. They acquire physical, behavioral, social, and contextual priors that shape imagined futures.

6.6 From Prediction to Understanding
The central message of this analysis is that prediction and understanding are not equivalent. A model
may predict plausible futures while relying on brittle, biased, or poorly calibrated priors. Conversely,
a model may have modest prediction accuracy but encode useful assumptions about hidden state,
intent, interaction, or context.
DISCRETIO therefore reframes world-model evaluation. Instead of asking only:
Did the model predict the future correctly?
we ask:
What assumptions made that future likely?

This shift matters because predictive world models are increasingly used in embodied agents,
planning systems, simulation engines, and decision-making pipelines. In such settings, risk often
comes not from visibly implausible predictions, but from plausible rollouts produced by incorrect
hidden assumptions.
By exposing these assumptions, DISCRETIO moves world-model evaluation from prediction assessment to prior discovery. The result is a more diagnostic view of world-model behavior: not only what
futures a model predicts, but what kind of world it implicitly assumes it is predicting.

7 Discussion and Future Directions
DISCRETIO reframes world-model evaluation around a simple but consequential shift: from asking
whether a model predicts plausible futures to asking what assumptions make those futures plausible.
The preceding sections show that predictive world models encode structured latent behavioral priors
over persistence, intent, interaction, and context. This section discusses what this shift implies for
evaluation, embodied AI, safety, and future world-model research.

7.1 From Prediction to Understanding
Modern world-model evaluation is largely built around a single question:
Can the model predict the future?
This question is important, but incomplete. Predictive performance captures what future is produced, but not why that future emerges. Two models can generate similarly accurate futures while
relying on fundamentally different hidden assumptions.
DISCRETIO therefore separates three complementary dimensions of world-model behavior:
1. Prediction: Does the model generate plausible futures?
2. Prior Sensitivity: Does the model respond to meaningful hidden factors?
3. Prior Calibration: Are those responses justified by real-world outcomes?
A model may excel on one dimension while failing on another. Thus, forecasting accuracy alone is not
a sufficient proxy for world understanding.

7.2 What DISCRETIO Adds
DISCRETIO makes latent behavioral priors observable through controlled interventions and rollout
comparisons. Its contribution is not merely another benchmark score, but a diagnostic framework for
asking:
What kind of world does the model assume when evidence is incomplete?
The framework provides four capabilities:
• Prior Discovery: exposing assumptions invisible to conventional forecasting metrics.
• Controlled Probing: isolating hidden variables while preserving observed dynamics.
• Sensitivity–Calibration Separation: distinguishing whether a model reacts from whether the
reaction is justified.
• Prior Profiling: characterizing models by their physical, behavioral, social, and contextual assumptions.
Together, these capabilities transform latent priors from an implicit property of world models into
a measurable object of study.

7.3 Implications for Embodied AI and Planning
World models are increasingly used inside embodied agents, autonomous systems, planning frameworks, and simulation engines. In these settings, agents do not act on observed reality alone; they act
on imagined futures. The quality of those futures depends directly on the quality of the priors that
generate them.
Incorrect priors can produce plausible but unsafe rollouts. A robot may over-preserve an occluded
agent that has left the scene. A planner may become overly conservative because of exaggerated
hesitation priors. A model may over-apply social-role expectations or transfer contextual behavior
across incompatible environments.
The danger is therefore not only implausible prediction. It is plausible prediction driven by
incorrect hidden assumptions. This is precisely the class of failure that DISCRETIO is designed to
expose.

7.4 Limitations
Several limitations remain.
First, DISCRETIO is constructed from approximately 700 hours of Indian suburban video. This setting provides rich ambiguity, occlusion, interaction, and contextual diversity, but it does not represent
the full range of global mobility cultures. The benchmark should therefore be viewed as a first step
toward broader cross-cultural prior evaluation.
Second, the benchmark relies on scenario reconstruction and counterfactual generation. Although
filtering and validation reduce artifacts, some generated variants may still introduce confounds.
Third, calibration depends on empirical outcome distributions. Rare events and long-tail interactions remain difficult to estimate reliably.
Fourth, some world models expose limited rollout uncertainty, restricting the evaluation of richer
belief structures.
Finally, DISCRETIO measures priors behaviorally through outputs. It reveals that a prior exists, but
not necessarily where or how it is represented internally.

7.5 Future Directions
DISCRETIO opens several directions for future work.
Cross-cultural prior discovery. Future versions can expand beyond Indian suburban scenes to
compare priors across cities, countries, traffic cultures, and mobility regimes.
Action-conditioned prior evaluation. Current probes focus on passive prediction. A natural extension is to study how latent priors influence planning, control, and intervention.
Mechanistic prior analysis. Combining DISCRETIO with interpretability methods may reveal where
physical, behavioral, social, and contextual priors are encoded inside world-model representations.
Prior-aware training and safety. Controlled probes can be used not only for evaluation, but also for
training models to improve calibration, reduce harmful over-generalization, and detect unsafe hidden
assumptions before deployment.

8 Conclusion
Predictive world models are no longer just tools for forecasting. They are becoming the substrate for
planning, simulation, embodied control, and decision making. In such settings, the critical question
is not only:
What future does the model predict?
but also:
What assumptions make that future plausible?

We introduced DISCRETIO, a benchmark and evaluation framework for discovering latent behavioral
priors in predictive world models. Through controlled interventions and sensitivity–calibration analysis, DISCRETIO reveals whether models encode assumptions about physical persistence, behavioral
intent, social interaction, and environmental context.

The larger lesson is simple but important:
• Prediction is not understanding.
• Sensitivity is not calibration.
• Plausible futures can still be driven by brittle priors.
• World models must be evaluated not only by what they predict, but by what they assume.
This shift matters for the future of embodied AI. A robot, planner, or simulator does not act on pixels
alone; it acts on imagined futures. If those futures are shaped by incorrect hidden assumptions, the
resulting behavior may appear reasonable while remaining unsafe, biased, or poorly calibrated.
The central message of this paper is that world models do not merely predict the future; they
implicitly construct theories about the world they predict. By making those theories observable,
measurable, and auditable, DISCRETIO moves world-model evaluation beyond prediction assessment
toward a deeper science of predictive intelligence.
